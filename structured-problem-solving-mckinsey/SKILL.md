# Structured Problem Solving (McKinsey Method)

## When to Use
- Facing complex business problems requiring structured solutions and analysis frameworks
- Building initial hypotheses to guide problem-solving roadmaps
- Risk of over-analyzing data or applying cookie-cutter solutions
- Need to ensure analysis covers all critical factors without overlap or omission

## Core Workflow

### 1. Apply MECE Principle to Structure Problems
Decompose problems into **M**utually **E**xclusive, **C**ollectively **E**xhaustive components:

- **Verify Mutual Exclusivity**: Ensure sub-items have no overlap (no element belongs to multiple categories)
- **Verify Collective Exhaustiveness**: Ensure sub-items cover 100% of the parent issue with no gaps
- **Select Decomposition Method**:
  - **Binary**: Divide into A vs. Non-A (e.g., domestic/international, online/offline)
  - **Process**: Follow chronological steps (e.g., R&D → Production → Sales → Service)
  - **Components**: Use constituent elements (e.g., 4P marketing mix)
  - **Formula**: Apply mathematical relationships (e.g., Revenue = Volume × Price)
- **Limit Scope**: Maintain 3-7 sub-items per level; avoid excessive granularity
- **Avoid Common Errors**: Do not use "Other" as a large category, do not mix decomposition standards at the same level, and do not over-decompose

### 2. Build and Validate Initial Hypotheses
Create solution roadmaps before full analysis begins:

**Generate the Hypothesis**:
1. **Understand Context**: Spend 1-2 hours reviewing industry publications and consulting internal experts to grasp terminology and trends
2. **Decompose Problem**: Identify key drivers using MECE structure
3. **Formulate Actionable Recommendations**: Propose specific, testable recommendations for each driver (e.g., replace "pray for good weather" with "improve climate resilience capabilities")
4. **Decompose into Issues**: Break recommendations into testable sub-issues by asking "If this recommendation is correct, what questions must be true?"
5. **Plan Analysis**: Determine exactly what data is needed to prove or disprove each issue; use team experience to avoid dead ends

**Construct Issue Tree**: Visualize the hypothesis and its decomposition as a logical tree structure serving as the problem-solving roadmap.

**Validate Through Teamwork**:
- Conduct self-check: Is this the best possible hypothesis? Does it cover all key drivers? Are all recommendations feasible and testable?
- Convene validation meeting with 3-4 intelligent team members (avoid solo validation due to confirmation bias)
- Structure meeting: Each member presents insights; leader pushes thinking forward using "what if" scenarios
- Allow unconventional ideas to stimulate thinking

### 3. Avoid Over-Analysis ("Boiling the Ocean")
Stop data collection when sufficient to validate hypotheses:

- **Work Smart, Not Hard**: Ignore most available data; collect only to prove or disprove specific hypotheses
- **Apply Sufficiency Standard**: In business contexts, "adequate facts" meet requirements—perfection is not required
- **Implement Stop Mechanism**: When analysis is "good enough" to make decisions, stop immediately
- **Prioritize Ruthlessly**: Focus only on priority issues; do not attempt comprehensive analysis of all aspects
- **Recognize Waste**: Excessive fact-finding consumes precious time for minimal return (like boiling the ocean to extract salt)

## Key Principles
- **Hypotheses Are Not Answers**: They are theories to be proven or disproven through fact-based analysis
- **Trust But Verify**: Even when experience suggests intuition is correct, validate with facts before concluding
- **Actionable Only**: All recommendations must be feasible and verifiable; avoid untestable suggestions

## Output
A structured Issue Tree with validated initial hypotheses, clear analysis plans, and defined stopping criteria for data collection.
